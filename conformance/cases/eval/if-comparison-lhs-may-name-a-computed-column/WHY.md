# Why this case exists

A `comparison`'s left-hand `ident` may name a computed column **when the
right-hand side is numeric**, and §4.2 rule 10 says so in as many words: "A
**numeric** right-hand side carries no such restriction: `if(total = 20, …)` and
`if(total > 15, …)` over a computed column are well-formed and rule 9 gives them
a value."

`total` is computed. `total > 15` and `total = 20` compare **numbers**, and a
computed column has a number under rule 9, so both are `1` for `r_01` and `0`
for `r_02`. Both columns are in the same file so an implementation cannot pass
by being consistently wrong about one of them.

Rule 10 states the failure this guards against, and states it as a warning
rather than a possibility: "Comparing `if` over the column one cell to its left
is the second most common shape in the corpus, and over-applying §9.22 to every
`ident` in every comparison — one line, and it looks like defence in depth —
refuses it."

**The `string` variant of this file is now refused, and this case is the reason
the refusal exists.** When it was written, `if(total = "20", 1, 0)` was
generated by the grammar, unreachable by §9.20, and out of scope for §9.22 —
which was worded for `where` predicates only — so the format had a construct
with two readings and no rule, both producing a number. §9.22 has since been
widened to cover it and rule 10 now carries the measurement: `total` of `20`
renders `20.0` in the reference implementation and never matches `"20"`, so the
comparison was false in every row and said nothing, while a reader that renders
`20` would match every row.

`parse/if-string-equality-against-a-computed-column-refused` and its two
siblings are that refusal;
`parse/if-string-equality-against-a-stored-column-accepted` is the boundary on
the other side.
